# Character/Subject Style — The Voynich Manuscript

Several scenes call for human figures: Rudolf II's court, Marci and Kircher,
the Jesuit sale, Wilfrid Voynich, WWII-era codebreakers, and the
manuscript's own illustrated "nymph" figures. All must share one rendering
approach.

## Rendering approach

- **Painterly semi-realistic illustration**, not photorealism and not flat
  cartoon/vector. Think restrained oil-painting or period-etching
  portraiture — visible brushwork-like texture, soft edges, believable
  anatomy, but not hyper-detailed or photo-referenced.
- Faces are **often partially obscured** — turned away, in profile, lit from
  one side with the other in Shadow Charcoal, or softly out of focus in the
  background — to preserve the documentary's mystery tone and avoid the
  video looking like a cast of named "characters." No figure should ever
  become a recurring cartoon mascot.
- Flesh tones are desaturated into the palette (muted Umber/Cream mixes,
  never bright pink/tan) so figures visually belong to the same world as the
  parchment and ink.
- Clothing and setting details should be historically plausible for each
  era (Renaissance Prague court dress for Rudolf II's scenes, Jesuit robes
  for Kircher/Marci, Edwardian suit and mustache for Wilfrid Voynich, 1940s
  military/civilian dress for the codebreaker montage) but rendered with the
  same restrained painterly treatment — not a costume-drama level of
  crispness.

## The manuscript's own illustrated figures ("nymphs")

- When depicting the biological-section "nymphs," follow the manuscript's
  own illustrated, non-literal style — stylized, small-scale figures
  integrated into the tube/vessel diagrams, treated as archival illustration
  content rather than dramatized human subjects.
- Tasteful and documentary in nature: figures are simplified and
  illustrative, not detailed or sexualized, consistent with reproducing a
  historical museum artifact for an educational audience.

## What to avoid

- No modern hairstyles, makeup, dental work, or expressions that read as
  contemporary.
- No named "cast" continuity — a figure appearing in one scene doesn't need
  to visually recur in another; treat each as a fresh illustrated tableau.
- No crowds or busy backgrounds — one to three figures per scene, max, to
  keep focus on the manuscript and its mystery rather than on people.
