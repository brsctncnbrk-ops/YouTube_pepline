# The Voynich Manuscript — Research Summary

## Summary

The Voynich Manuscript is a roughly 240-page illustrated codex written entirely
in an unknown script ("Voynichese") that has resisted every attempt at
decipherment for over a century. Radiocarbon dating places its vellum firmly
in the early 15th century (1404–1438), ruling out the modern-hoax theories
that once surrounded it. Its documented history begins with Holy Roman
Emperor Rudolf II in Prague around 1600, passes through Prague alchemists and
Jesuit scholars including Athanasius Kircher, then goes dark for over two
centuries until Jesuit priests near Rome quietly sold it in 1912 to rare-book
dealer Wilfrid Voynich, whose name it now bears. Since 1969 it has lived at
Yale's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library as MS 408. The book is
divided into six thematic sections — herbal, astronomical, biological,
cosmological, pharmaceutical, and recipes — filled with drawings of
unidentifiable plants, naked female figures bathing in green liquid through
tubed structures, and starred marginal text, but not a single word of the
underlying script has ever been reliably translated. The text's statistical
structure looks like real language, which is exactly why the hoax theory and
the genuine-lost-language theory continue to compete, and why the manuscript
remains, by consensus, the world's most famous undeciphered text.

## Key Facts

- The manuscript's vellum was radiocarbon-dated by University of Arizona
  researchers to 1404–1438 with 95% confidence, from four separate samples. (s6)
- The book contains roughly 240 surviving pages in an unknown script now
  called "Voynichese." (s1)
- Six sections by illustration style: herbal, astronomical,
  biological/balneological, cosmological, pharmaceutical, and recipes. (s5)
- The Herbal section spans 126 pages, each with one or two plants and
  accompanying text — but no plant has been unambiguously identified. (s5)
- The Biological section depicts small nude female figures ("nymphs")
  connected by tubes to organ-like or bath-like vessels — unlike anything in
  comparable medieval manuscripts. (s5)
- Earliest documented owner: likely Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II of Prague,
  who purchased it for 600 gold ducats around 1600. (s4)
- In 1665/1666, Prague scholar Jan Marek Marci sent it to Jesuit polymath
  Athanasius Kircher in Rome, hoping he could decipher it. He failed. (s4)
- The manuscript then disappears from the record for roughly two centuries
  before resurfacing in Jesuit hands near Rome. (s4)
- Facing financial pressure, the Jesuits discreetly sold a batch of rare
  manuscripts around 1911–1912, brokered in secrecy. (s4)
- Wilfrid Michael Voynich acquired the manuscript in 1912; it has carried his
  name ever since. (s4)
- Hans P. Kraus donated the manuscript to Yale in 1969; catalogued as
  Beinecke MS 408. (s1)
- In 2020, Yale published complete high-resolution scans online, free for
  anyone to study. (s1)
- Statistical/information-theoretic analysis shows word-frequency patterns
  consistent with genuine natural language — part of why the hoax theory
  remains contested rather than settled. (s3)
- No decipherment claim — including a widely publicized 2019 claim of a
  Hebrew basis, and various 2020s AI/machine-learning attempts — has produced
  a translation accepted by the scholarly community. (s2)
- A proposed cipher system called "Naibbe" can generate text with
  statistical properties similar to Voynichese, offering one plausible (but
  unproven) mechanical explanation for the script. (s2)

## Timeline

- **1404–1438** — Radiocarbon-dated creation window of the vellum. (s6)
- **c. 1600** — Believed purchase by Emperor Rudolf II in Prague for 600
  ducats. (s4)
- **1666** — Jan Marek Marci sends the manuscript to Athanasius Kircher in
  Rome with a letter recounting its history. (s4)
- **17th century – early 1900s** — Held in the library of the Collegio
  Romano / Jesuit order, largely out of public view. (s4)
- **1911–1912** — Financially pressured Jesuits quietly sell a batch of rare
  manuscripts. (s4)
- **1912** — Wilfrid Voynich acquires the manuscript. (s4)
- **1969** — Hans P. Kraus donates it to Yale University; catalogued as
  Beinecke MS 408. (s1)
- **2009–2011** — University of Arizona team carbon-dates the vellum. (s6)
- **2020** — Yale publishes complete high-resolution digital scans online. (s1)
- **2020s** — Renewed decipherment attempts via statistical linguistics and
  AI; none yet accepted as a solution. (s2)

## Open Questions

- Whether Voynich acquired the manuscript at Villa Mondragone (traditional
  account) or Villa Torlonia in Castel Gandolfo (revisionist recent
  scholarship) is unresolved.
- No plant, diagram, or figure in the manuscript has been conclusively
  identified — any claim about "what the illustrations show" is scholarly
  interpretation, not settled fact.
- Whether Voynichese encodes a real (if unknown) language, a constructed
  language, a cipher over a known language, or is meaningless hoax text
  remains genuinely unresolved among specialists. This is the central open
  mystery and should not be resolved in favor of any one theory in the script.
- Individual decipherment claims (e.g., the 2019 Hebrew-basis claim) have
  been publicly disputed by other researchers and should be attributed to
  their proponents, not presented as accepted findings.
