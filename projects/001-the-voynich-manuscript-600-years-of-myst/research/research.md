# Research — The Voynich Manuscript: 600 Years of Mystery

## Summary

The Voynich Manuscript (Beinecke MS 408) is an illustrated codex written in an
unknown, undeciphered script. Radiocarbon dating places its vellum to
1404-1438 CE. It contains roughly 240 surviving pages organized into
recognizable sections — botanical, astronomical/astrological, biological,
pharmaceutical/recipe, and a final text-heavy section — all illustrated with
plants, star charts, and figures that don't match any known species or
convention. Its documented ownership traces back to Emperor Rudolf II of
Bohemia, who reportedly paid 600 gold ducats for it and believed it was the
work of 13th-century friar Roger Bacon. It resurfaced in 1912 when
antiquarian book dealer Wilfrid Voynich acquired it from the Jesuit College at
Frascati, and it has resided at Yale's Beinecke Rare Book & Manuscript Library
since 1969. Numerous attempts to decode it — including a sustained
multi-decade effort by WWII codebreakers William and Elizabeth Friedman —
have failed to produce a scholarly-accepted translation.

## Key Facts

- Radiocarbon dating (University of Arizona, 2011) dates the vellum to 1404-1438 CE.
- Catalogued at Yale's Beinecke Library as MS 408.
- Documented provenance traces to Emperor Rudolf II, who believed it was Roger Bacon's work.
- Wilfrid M. Voynich acquired it in 1912 from the Jesuit College at Frascati.
- Donated to Yale's Beinecke Library in 1969 by H. P. Kraus.
- WWII codebreakers William and Elizabeth Friedman spent decades studying it, concluding it likely represents a constructed language.
- The text uses ~25 distinct glyphs with unusual positional rules.
- Pages are informally grouped into botanical, astronomical, biological, pharmaceutical, and text-only sections.
- None of the botanical illustrations reliably match real, identifiable plant species.
- Yale published full high-resolution digital scans online (2004, expanded 2020).

## Open Questions

- No scholarly-accepted translation exists — any "decoded" claim is contested, not settled.
- Natural language vs. constructed language vs. cipher vs. hoax remains debated.
- The original author/scribe's identity is unknown; Roger Bacon authorship is a later attribution, not an established fact.
